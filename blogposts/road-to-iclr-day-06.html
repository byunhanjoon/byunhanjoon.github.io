Road to ICLR · Day 6 / 30

Names shouldn't matter.
Neighbors should.

A model should ignore arbitrary IDs such as “Monday = 4.” But if Monday is missing from training, knowing that it sits beside Sunday and Tuesday can be genuinely useful.

August 29, 2026Han Joon ByunExperiments and results ↗

Day 5 treated column order, category IDs, and training seeds as accidents to average away. Today I pushed the same principle deeper: from the final prediction, through the model's internal table, down to the arithmetic that moves numbers around.

The two-contract test. A representation is trustworthy when arbitrary names do not matter, yet meaningful relationships still do.

An ID is not a location

Computers store categories as numbers. That does not make those numbers geometry. Hours 23 and 0 are neighbors on a clock; two diagnoses may be siblings in a medical hierarchy; product 117 and product 118 may have nothing in common. Subtracting their IDs answers the wrong question.

I first asked whether a neural network discovers this structure by itself. Mostly, it did not. Its learned category table became slightly more aligned with the true geometry, but not enough to pass the preset test. The better route was to provide the relationship directly.

Borrow from the right neighbors

The surviving idea is a Metric Partition Embedding (MPE). Place a small set of landmarks around the feature's real space. For any value, turn its distances to those landmarks into soft weights, then blend their learned tokens. A familiar value learns normally. An unseen value can borrow from nearby states.

An earlier version patched only missing rows in an already-trained category table. It improved unseen-state error in all 9 structured cases and degraded perfectly as I gradually corrupted the metric. MPE turns that one-off repair into the input layer itself.

The clean result—and the honest boundary

On synthetic circles and trees, test states were absent from training and the target varied smoothly over the true geometry. Against a standard numerical embedding, MPE reduced neural-model error by 97.3% on the circle and 62.3% on the tree, winning all 12 schema-and-seed comparisons in each domain. Deliberately scrambling the metric made performance worse every time.

The large wins are mechanism evidence, not a benchmark victory: the synthetic tasks were designed to reward the supplied geometry. On real hourly bike demand, the correct ring still beat a scrambled ring in all 6 runs, but simple Fourier clock features beat MPE in both model families. When every hour is observed and the topology is a clean cycle, the specialized tool is better.

A nominal-data control caught another false story. MPE initially appeared 77.6% better even when categories had no neighborhood at all. The cause was mundane: the comparison method sometimes merged discrete states. Once every state received its own slot, the “gain” fell to 0.094%. Resolution, not geometry, explained it.

What survived Day 6

01Ignore the name

Equivalent relabellings should produce the same representation, not merely similar average accuracy.

02Use real proximity

For an unseen state, borrow from neighbors defined by the feature—not by accidental integer IDs.

03Keep the boundary

MPE is for trustworthy, irregular metrics and missing states. It is not a universal replacement for simpler encoders.

Day 6 conclusion. The promising object is not a clever code. It is a contract: names are arbitrary; neighborhoods are not. MPE makes that contract explicit and testable—but its next proof must come from real unseen products, locations, hierarchy nodes, or device states whose metric is known before seeing the target.

The arithmetic experiments reinforce the same lesson at a lower level. More precise accumulation reduced damage from equivalent rewrites in all 51 long-run comparisons, but did not improve average predictive accuracy. Invariance is valuable evidence; it is not, by itself, usefulness.
