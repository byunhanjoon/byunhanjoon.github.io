Road to ICLR · Day 3 / 30

Same information,
different difficulty

An invertible change of coordinates cannot remove information or shrink a neural network’s function class. It can still make the same learning problem dramatically harder.

August 26, 2026Han Joon ByunExperiments and results ↗

Day 2 ended with a puzzle. Cumulative and local numerical bases span the same piecewise-linear functions, but they lead neural networks down different training paths. Blending those paths helped. That left a more basic question: was this a small encoder curiosity, or a general failure of optimization?

The question. If I deliberately make an information-equivalent basis ill-conditioned—while preserving its rank, scale, split, and represented functions—does neural tabular learning reliably get worse?

What a condition number feels like

Imagine a round cloud of points. A reversible coordinate change can stretch it into a thin ellipse without adding, removing, or merging a single point. The condition number, κ, measures the ratio between the most stretched and least stretched directions.

The controlled experiment starts from a whitened representation X1 and constructs X1000 = X1B, where B is invertible. A first affine layer can absorb B−1, so both inputs permit exactly the same predictors. The transformation preserves overall activation energy; it changes anisotropy, not global scale.

A broad causal signal

I first froze 25 available tabular tasks, four architectures, both κ endpoints, and three seeds. After that screen finished, I separately froze five additional datasets as a prospective replication. The two stages must be reported separately: the full 30 were not jointly preregistered before any outcome existed.

The effect survived every backbone. These are dense-stem versions of FT-Transformer and TabM so that an arbitrary full input-basis map remains an exact first-layer reparameterization; I do not call this native tokenization invariance. All 2,400 original broad cells and 120 extension cells completed without failures, and reconstruction errors stayed near 10−14.

What the result says. The function class stayed fixed, but finite training did not. Ordinary AdamW systematically assigned different difficulty to exactly equivalent descriptions of the same tabular problem.

Remove the arbitrary coordinates

The cleanest diagnostic was canonicalization. Center the training matrix, choose a deterministic set of linearly independent anchor rows, and describe every row using coefficients of those anchors. If X = CR, then after any invertible recoding B:

XB = C(RB)

The table and anchors both move, but the coefficient matrix C does not. It is like describing locations relative to landmarks: stretch the map and the landmarks together, and the landmark coefficients remain unchanged.

In ten datasets, four models, five seeds, and both endpoints, exact anchor canonicalization removed 99.81% of the paired sensitivity while losing 0.20% on the clean basis. A progressively sketched version removed 99.43%. A first-layer input-natural update—multiplying by the inverse input second moment and using compatible initialization—removed 99.69%.

The reality check

κ=1000 is a causal stress test, not a claim about the usual severity of deployed schemas. I therefore tested naturally named cumulative/local encodings and ordinary preprocessing choices. Across 150 natural pairs, the median absolute normalized gap was only 0.38%; its clustered interval included zero. Raw-standard, quantile-standard, and PLE differences were also small and lacked a universal ranking.

The time-split result is especially useful: deployment shift did not amplify the basis effect here; it mostly removed it. This is a limitation, not an argument for random splitting. The random split may mix entities and neighboring dates.

Rank stress tests add another caveat. Duplicating columns did not crash any of 504 runs, but an input-natural ridge of 10−10 produced erratic predictions on Microsoft as the preconditioner condition approached 1010. Ridges around 10−8 to 10−6 were safer. Exact anchors remained stable; the sketch could switch a near-tied pivot in floating point and was slower to fit than the full construction on this benchmark.

What is—and is not—the paper

Natural gradient, K-FAC’s ideal affine invariance,[1] whitening, Shampoo, SOAP, and canonicalization are prior art. Feature orientation in tabular learning was also studied before.[2] General canonicalization has known continuity limitations.[3] The result cannot honestly be sold as the invention of invariant optimization or the first observation that coordinates matter.

FINAL DAY 3 VERDICT ICLR-plausible, but borderline.

The potential contribution is the exact-equivalence causal benchmark: controlled conditioning, verified cross-schema orbits, prospective replication, modern backbones, canonical closure, and a practical audit of accuracy, rank, shift, runtime, and memory. The controlled evidence is strong. The natural effects and method novelty are modest.

The narrow claim is now stronger than Day 2’s ensemble observation:

Day 3 conclusion. Modern neural tabular learners show broad finite-training sensitivity within exactly equivalent representation orbits. Known invariance principles can close the gap, but practical approximations carry real stability, performance, and compute tradeoffs.
