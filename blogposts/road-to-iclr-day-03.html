Road to ICLR · Day 3 / 30

Same information,
different difficulty

A small encoding change raised Adult accuracy from 85.98% to 87.27%. The attempt to explain that jump uncovered a broader problem: two tables can say exactly the same thing and still be very different to learn from.

August 26, 2026Han Joon ByunExperiments and results ↗

Day 2 found the sort of result that is exciting and dangerous. I kept the Adult split, model, and training budget fixed, but gave capital gain and capital loss an exact-value identity view. Mean accuracy rose from 85.98% to 87.27%: a gain of 1.29 percentage points.

That is large enough to tempt a quick story. Perhaps zero is special. Perhaps exact values contain information that a smooth numerical encoder throws away. Or perhaps the model simply finds one coordinate system easier to use than another.

The controls weakened the simplest explanation. Indicators for gain = 0 and loss = 0 produced essentially no gain. Keeping only one, two, or four common values also did little. Improvement began around eight retained values and kept growing through dozens of levels. This looks less like one magic point mass and more like a distributed collection of exceptions.

Then came the more surprising clue. On the observed Adult states, a carefully constructed identity basis and a PLE basis can reconstruct one another to roughly 10−14 error. They contain the same information and span the same functions. Yet their raw scores differ.

The question. Can coordinates alone make a fixed neural learning problem easier or harder—even when information, rank, scale, split, model, and represented functions stay unchanged?

What a condition number feels like

Imagine a round cloud of points. A reversible coordinate change can stretch it into a thin ellipse without adding, removing, or merging a single point. The condition number, κ, measures the ratio between the most stretched and least stretched directions.

The controlled experiment starts from a whitened representation X1 and constructs X1000 = X1B, where B is invertible. A first affine layer can absorb B−1, so both inputs permit exactly the same predictors. The transformation preserves overall activation energy; it changes anisotropy, not global scale.

Did geometry explain Adult?

For capital gain and loss, I made the comparison exact. The identity and PLE designs had the same centered rank and reconstructed each other on train, validation, and test. Then I progressively removed arbitrary coordinate differences.

This is the cleanest Day 3 finding, but it needs a careful boundary. It explains a 0.210-point controlled gap, not automatically all of the original 1.29-point improvement. The original model appended an additional view and also changed resolution, redundancy, locality, and regularization. Geometry is a major mechanism; it is not yet the whole Adult attribution.

A broad causal signal

I first froze 25 available tabular tasks, four architectures, both κ endpoints, and three seeds. After that screen finished, I separately froze five additional datasets as a prospective replication. The two stages must be reported separately: the full 30 were not jointly preregistered before any outcome existed.

The effect survived every backbone. These are dense-stem versions of FT-Transformer and TabM so that an arbitrary full input-basis map remains an exact first-layer reparameterization; I do not call this native tokenization invariance. All 2,400 original broad cells and 120 extension cells completed without failures, and reconstruction errors stayed near 10−14.

What the result says. The function class stayed fixed, but ordinary training did not. This first benchmark still mixed two causes: a coordinate change can alter the function produced at initialization, and it can alter every optimizer update afterward.

Same function, then one AdamW step

To separate those causes, I ran a second experiment on Adult, California Housing, and Diamonds with MLP and ResNet, five seeds, and a fixed budget of 200 updates. Every pair saw identical rows, labels, minibatches, and dropout masks. The only intervention was the input basis.

Ordinary initialization uses the same parameter distribution in both bases, but that does not produce the same starting function. So I added a function-matched arm. For X′ = XB, the first-layer weights start at W′0 = W0B−T. Before training, the two networks then agree to numerical precision.

The result is sharper than “conditioning hurts.” At κ=3000, function matching removed 94.66% of the original harm, with reductions from 76.6% to 98.3% in every dataset/model group. Initialization was therefore the dominant mechanism in this fixed-budget stress test.

But AdamW was still not coordinate-equivariant. Mean prediction drift was 5.8×10−6 at step 0 and 0.071 after one update; all 90 controlled matched cells moved apart. A matched input-natural update nearly closed the controlled experiment, with step-200 drift of 7.6×10−5 and endpoint harm of 1.6×10−7.

One hoped-for insight did not survive its frozen gate. One-step drift predicted endpoint harm only slightly better than log condition number (leave-one-dataset-out R2 −0.160 versus −0.201; improvement 0.041, below the required 0.10). Early drift is a detector of broken equivariance, not a universal measure of consequential damage. The task, labels, and local curvature still decide whether that drift matters.

Remove the arbitrary coordinates

The cleanest diagnostic was canonicalization. Center the training matrix, choose a deterministic set of linearly independent anchor rows, and describe every row using coefficients of those anchors. If X = CR, then after any invertible recoding B:

XB = C(RB)

The table and anchors both move, but the coefficient matrix C does not. It is like describing locations relative to landmarks: stretch the map and the landmarks together, and the landmark coefficients remain unchanged.

In ten datasets, four models, five seeds, and both endpoints, exact anchor canonicalization removed 99.81% of the paired sensitivity while losing 0.20% on the clean basis. A progressively sketched version removed 99.43%. A first-layer input-natural update—multiplying by the inverse input second moment and using compatible initialization—removed 99.69%.

The reality check

κ=1000 is a causal stress test, not a claim about the usual severity of deployed schemas. I therefore tested naturally named cumulative/local encodings and ordinary preprocessing choices. Across 150 natural pairs, the median absolute normalized gap was only 0.38%; its clustered interval included zero. Raw-standard, quantile-standard, and PLE differences were also small and lacked a universal ranking.

The time-split result is especially useful: deployment shift did not amplify the basis effect here; it mostly removed it. This is a limitation, not an argument for random splitting. The random split may mix entities and neighboring dates.

Rank stress tests add another caveat. Duplicating columns did not crash any of 504 runs, but an input-natural ridge of 10−10 produced erratic predictions on Microsoft as the preconditioner condition approached 1010. Ridges around 10−8 to 10−6 were safer. Exact anchors remained stable; the sketch could switch a near-tied pivot in floating point and was slower to fit than the full construction on this benchmark.

The trajectory experiment made that rank caveat concrete. Adult's natural basis had 123 columns but rank 116. Even with a perfectly conditioned basis map and relation error below 5×10−13, an undamped inverse amplified numerical noise in the seven null directions: matched input-natural drift reached 0.374 by step 200. California and Diamonds were full rank and closed to roughly 3×10−7. Invariance therefore needs an explicit effective-rank or damping policy; exact algebra alone is not enough.

The performance method failed its last test

I also tried to turn the mechanism into performance. Selective Measure-Orbit combined ordinary TabM with a representation chosen from the same equivalence orbit. It looked convincing internally: Adult gained 1.013 accuracy points in the one-fit comparison, and a frozen 21-dataset confirmation improved proper loss by 1.103% on average.

Then I tested it on seven untouched datasets against an exactly update-matched two-seed ordinary TabM ensemble. Measure-Orbit was 0.521% worse, with a 95% dataset-bootstrap interval of [−0.831%, −0.195%]. It won only 7 of 21 seed-level comparisons. The method is a useful diagnostic, not a general performance claim.

This changed how I read the internal success. Representation diversity may help, but a second ordinary seed is already a strong and simple use of the same compute. Any next method must face that baseline from the beginning.

What survived Day 3

FOUNDA causal phenomenon
  • Equivalent bases can change finite-budget learning.
  • The effect spans numerical, nominal, and ordinal blocks.
  • Adult’s exact same-space gap is mostly geometric.
  • Initialization explains most severe stress-test harm.
  • AdamW still breaks equivalence after one matched update.
NOT YET FOUNDA general practical cure
  • No universally best natural encoding.
  • No reliable early predictor of harmful drift.
  • No new invariant optimizer claim.
  • No method that beats a fair seed ensemble externally.
  • No complete attribution of Adult’s original +1.29 points.

What is—and is not—the paper

Natural gradient, K-FAC’s ideal affine invariance,[1] whitening, Shampoo, SOAP, and canonicalization are prior art. Feature orientation in tabular learning was also studied before.[2] General canonicalization has known continuity limitations.[3] The result cannot honestly be sold as the invention of invariant optimization, the first observation that coordinates matter, or a validated new performance method.

FINAL DAY 3 VERDICT A strong phenomenon and mechanism; an unfinished method story.

The defensible contribution is an exact-equivalence causal benchmark plus a function-matched mechanism decomposition: ordinary initialization explains most severe fixed-budget harm, AdamW diverges after one matched update, and invariant closure exposes a rank requirement. The Adult closure gives the story a concrete origin. Natural effects remain small, the universal predictor failed, and the performance method failed externally.

The next experiment: hold conditioning fixed

Condition number is now an established lever, but it is not a complete theory. Whitening can make every direction equally scaled while destroying locality: a sparse coordinate that means “this value” becomes a dense mixture of many values. Two bases can both have κ=1 and still feel very different to a finite neural network.

This is the clean next test. Start from a whitened local basis, rotate it gradually toward a dense random orthogonal basis, and function-match the first layer. Then measure the first 100 updates and final performance on Adult, Black Friday, and Diamond across MLP, ResNet, and TabM.

If performance changes smoothly with rotation density, locality becomes a mechanism rather than an intuition. If the gap vanishes, the simpler account wins: conditioning and initialization were enough.

What you can use now

When one tabular encoding beats another, do not ask only which name won. Ask what changed:

  1. Information: can each representation reconstruct the other on every split?
  2. Geometry: what are their rank, scale, singular values, and condition number?
  3. Coordinates: does the gap survive standardization, whitening, and alignment?
  4. Training: does it survive function-matched initialization?
  5. Compute: does the proposed method beat spending the same updates on another ordinary seed?
Day 3 conclusion. A table does not have one natural neural representation. Equivalent coordinates alter both the function prior induced by ordinary initialization and the optimizer trajectory that follows. Day 3 found a reliable way to expose that gap. The next step is to learn which useful properties of a coordinate system—conditioning, locality, sparsity, or initialization—we should preserve on purpose.
