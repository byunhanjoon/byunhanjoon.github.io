Road to ICLR · Day 2 / 30

Same information,
different path

Two encodings can contain the same information and span the same functions—yet guide a neural network toward different solutions. That difference can be useful.

August 25, 2026Han Joon ByunExperiments and results ↗

Day 1 found an odd result: piecewise-linear encoding (PLE)[1] improved tabular neural networks, but Adult improved further when two numerical columns also received exact-value identity views. I first treated this as a feature-selection problem. It turned out to be a clue about optimization.

The question. If two representations preserve the same values and the same function class, can their coordinates alone change what a tabular model learns?

Same states, different coordinates

On a finite set of supported values, cumulative PLE and one-hot identity are simply two coordinate systems. Click through the states below. PLE accumulates steps; identity moves one active coordinate.

Equivalent functions do not imply equivalent learning. Weight decay penalizes coordinates, and gradient descent moves through those coordinates. A state-local exception may need a long combination of cumulative features but only one identity feature.

Exact exceptions: real, but narrow

I fit a smooth PLE model, measured held-out residuals, and added an exact-state view only when it improved all five discovery folds. For pairs, I first removed both one-column effects: otherwise an additive pattern can masquerade as an interaction.

The intervention worked where the residual story predicted it: Adult had separate capital-gain and capital-loss exceptions; Black Friday had small joint-state effects; Miami Housing had one prospectively selected pair. The same view helped MLP, ResNet, and TabM, and 40 label-permutation controls produced zero discoveries.

Then the broad hypothesis failed. Before opening six untouched TabArena datasets,[3] I required discoveries on at least two. Only Miami passed: 1/6. Exact-state views are useful exceptions, not a universal encoder. This failure suggested a better generalization: keep the idea of equivalent coordinates, but stop requiring exact repeated states.

From exact states to local coordinates

Standard PLE uses cumulative ramps. I formed an equivalent local basis by taking adjacent differences of those ramps. With a bias term, both span the same piecewise-linear functions and use the same feature dimension. But the local basis activates at most two coordinates for any value.

Neither basis was universally better alone. Their errors were different enough to ensemble. For each run, I trained the same parameter-matched MLP or ResNet once in each basis, then chose a convex blend on validation loss. The test set remained untouched.

The control matters. A second random seed is slightly stronger overall, especially on House, Microsoft, and Otto. Basis diversity is not free magic. But it beats seed diversity on Adult, Black Friday, California, Churn, and Diamond—and local-only and concatenated-basis models fail to explain the result. The benefit comes from two fitted paths, not from one superior representation.

Day 2 conclusion. A numerical encoding is not merely a container for information. It is a coordinate system for optimization. Information-equivalent coordinates can produce complementary errors, making basis choice an ensemble hyperparameter.

This is narrower than claiming a universally better encoder, but broader than the original identity trick. The next question is whether the effect survives a preregistered TabArena panel, stronger hyperparameter ensembles, and models beyond MLP and ResNet—and whether training can become invariant to these changes without paying for two models.
