Road to ICLR · Day 5 / 30

Average the orbit,
not the accident

Harmless schema rewrites and random training seeds create 128 versions of one pipeline. Sixteen training runs chosen to balance every pair can estimate their full average better than sixteen random draws.

August 28, 2026Han Joon ByunExperiments and results ↗

Day 3 found that harmless rewrites of a table can move its predictions. Day 4 tried to profit from that disagreement by replacing one seed in an ensemble with another representation.

On a second dataset group fixed in advance, HeteroBag—two numerical encodings whose breakpoints follow target patterns plus one based on equal-frequency bins or ranks—won 56 of 72 comparisons and reduced loss by 2.41%. But across three preset seeds, a harmless coordinate transform gained 1.71% while the meaningful alternate view gained 1.15%. Diversity helped; semantic diversity was not isolated.

The Day 5 pivot. Stop asking which arbitrary representation should win. Declare which changes should not matter, then estimate the prediction averaged over all of them.

The accident is a product, not a knob

Consider four operations that preserve a classification problem’s meaning: reorder columns, rename category codes, swap the numerical codes for class labels and realign the probabilities, and change the random seed controlling initialization. These are nuisances: choices that may change the fitted model but should not change the question. They form a 4 × 4 × 2 × 4 grid—128 versions of one training pipeline, meaning the complete preprocessing and model-fitting procedure. I call this set of equivalent versions its orbit.

This is broader than seed variance and stricter than data augmentation: every rewrite passes through the entire training pipeline, and class probabilities are aligned back before comparison. For squared probability error (Brier loss) and ordinary squared error, a member’s average excess loss over the quotient is exactly its prediction variance across the 128 choices.

The audit found measurable schema risk—prediction variance caused only by equivalent rewrites—on all 16 datasets, while a linear model built to ignore those rewrites had none. An exact variance decomposition showed substantial interactions between choices. That explains why averaging one nuisance at a time worked in only 2 of 18 non-negligible cases.

Sixteen training runs can cover more than sixteen draws

Sixteen independent random draws (IID) are fair on average, but in one run they can repeat some combinations and miss others. A pair-balanced cover, called strength 2 in experimental design, deliberately chooses 16 of the 128 variants so every possible pair of choices appears equally often. Effects involving one or two choices then cancel; only combinations of three or more survive.

The result survived different rewrites, seeds, row subsets, public benchmark datasets, and both classification and regression. Across these related repeats, pair balance beat random sampling in all 57 cases with measurable schema risk. This is an audit count, not 57 independent trials.

The 4/16/64 hierarchy says to balance interactions up to the order that matters. It is not a guarantee: if an effect appears only when three nuisance choices meet, pair balance can be 16/9 times as risky as random sampling. The design must match the interaction structure.

An unbiased prediction can still have a biased score

A finite cover prediction is unbiased: averaged over repeated covers, it equals the full 128-model prediction. Its ordinary squared loss is still too high on average because remaining prediction noise enters the square. Two independently drawn covers remove that extra term. If their predictions are Q̂A and Q̂B, and y is the label, multiply their errors element by element and average:

balanced set A〈 y − Q̂A, y − Q̂B 〉balanced set Bcorrect on average for the full-orbit Brier loss or mean squared error

With 32 training runs, this score’s root-mean-square error (RMSE) was lower in 158 of 171 model candidates. Dataset-group averages were only 27–38% of an equally budgeted, unbiased score built from random draws; calculated and measured variance agreed within about 4%.

Balanced sets can also be made disjoint: pair sets sharing no model variant, or pack four into 64 training runs, so their errors tend to cancel. Negative coupling itself is classical.[4] Here it operates on a finite grid of exact pipeline rewrites. On four previously unseen datasets and five model types, all 17 nontrivial cases improved; four-set packing cut scoring error by 19.47%.

The better selector can still lose

Lower score noise made validation rankings more faithful everywhere. Yet on an eight-dataset test fixed in advance, agreement with the exact validation winner rose from 90.5% to 95.8% while selected test loss worsened. The exact validation and test winners agreed on only 2 of 8 datasets.

A noisy selector can accidentally choose the test-preferred model; a better one faithfully chooses the validation-preferred model. Across alternate splits, candidate gaps moved typically 55.3 times more than the remaining scoring error. Once schema-and-seed sampling is nearly solved, data-split instability dominates.

What survived Day 5

01Choose the target first

Evaluate the average over all declared equivalent schemas and seeds—not whichever version happened to run.

02Balance known structure

Spend training runs covering interactions. Balance individual choices, pairs, or triples according to the measured variance decomposition.

03Respect the boundary

Lower schema-and-seed variance improves evaluation. It cannot make one finite data split rank models correctly.

Day 5 conclusion. Exact schema rewrites and random seeds should be averaged together. OrbitCover balances a fixed set of variants, aligns and averages their predictions, then uses two independent balanced sets when an honest validation score is needed.

This is an evaluation and model-selection paper, not a prediction record. The next test needs new datasets and repeated splits, because the dominant uncertainty now comes from the data split.
