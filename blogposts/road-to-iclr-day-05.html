Road to ICLR · Day 5 / 30

Average the orbit,
not the accident

A table’s exact spellings and training seeds form one nuisance space. Sixteen deliberately balanced fits can estimate its 128-member average better than sixteen random draws.

August 28, 2026Han Joon ByunExperiments and results ↗

Day 3 found that harmless rewrites of a table can move its predictions. Day 4 tried to profit from that disagreement by replacing one seed in an ensemble with another representation.

The performance signal grew on a conditional second panel: HeteroBag won 56 of 72 comparisons and reduced loss by 2.41% on average. Then the decisive control broke the story. On the frozen mechanism triplet, replacing the third member with a merely transformed T-PLE model gained 1.71%; the supposedly meaningful alternate representation gained 1.15%. Diversity helped, but the experiment did not isolate semantic diversity.

The Day 5 pivot. Stop asking which arbitrary representation should win. Declare which changes should not matter, then estimate the prediction averaged over all of them.

The accident is a product, not a knob

Consider four operations that preserve the meaning of a classification problem: reorder features, rename category IDs, swap target IDs and align the probabilities back, and change the training seed. In the main construction they create a 4 × 4 × 2 × 4 product: 128 fitted versions of one learning pipeline.

This is broader than seed variance and stricter than data augmentation: every action passes through the entire pipeline, and outputs are aligned back to the same semantic classes before comparison. For Brier loss and squared error, the average member’s excess loss over this quotient is exactly the prediction variance across the product.

The audit found material schema risk somewhere on all 16 datasets, while the invariant one-hot linear control had none. Adam MLP was material on 16/16, ordinal forest on 13/16, CatBoost on 7/16, and HistGradientBoosting on 3/16. More importantly, exact prediction-space fANOVA found substantial pair and higher-order interactions. That explains why the first remedy—average one nuisance at a time—worked in only 2 of 18 material cases.

Sixteen fits can cover more than sixteen draws

A random 16-member ensemble is marginally fair, but it can repeatedly sample some factor pairs and miss others. A strength-2 cover spends the same 16 fits so that every pair of nuisance levels appears equally often. Main effects and pair interactions cancel by construction; only higher-order structure survives.

The result survived new nuisance generators, a changed subsample, external OpenML datasets, and a task-balanced classification/regression panel. Across these dependent repeats, strength-2 beat IID and strength-1 in all 57 material cells; it beat seed blocks in 55. Those counts are a descriptive audit, not 57 independent trials.

The increasing 4/16/64 hierarchy is more revealing than a single win. It says to match design strength to interaction order. It does not say orthogonal arrays always win: a pure triple interaction makes the 16-fit strength-2 design 16/9 times as risky as IID. Later high-dimensional tests found this boundary in real data. The method works when the nuisance spectrum matches the cover.

An unbiased prediction can still have a biased score

A finite cover prediction is unbiased for the quotient. Its ordinary squared loss is not: residual variance pushes the score upward. Two independent covers remove that term. If their predictions are Q̂A and Q̂B, score the cross-product

cover A〈 y − Q̂A, y − Q̂B 〉cover Bexactly unbiased for full-quotient Brier loss or MSE

This small algebraic move produced the cleanest mechanism result. At 32 fits, the cover cross-score had lower quotient-loss RMSE in 158 of 171 candidates. Its panel-average RMSE was only 27–38% of the equally budgeted IID U-statistic. A separate covariance audit predicted the measured variance to within a geometric ratio of 0.988–1.042 across every panel and method.

The covers can also be made deliberately disjoint. Pair two covers that avoid one another, or pack four disjoint covers into 64 fits. On small products this closes to the exact quotient; before closure it creates negative dependence. Antithetic risk estimation is not new—2026 work already proves variance gains for antithetic Gaussian cross-validation.[4] The narrower distinction here is finite, discrete, and interaction-aware.

In the final untouched four-source, five-model-family block, every one of 17 nondegenerate candidates improved under each packing action. The four-pack cut score RMSE by 19.47%. Across the later 15-source synthesis, every source remained favorable, with a 19.39% equal-source reduction—the synthesis is post-outcome, while the four-source gate itself was prospective.

The better selector can still lose

Lower score noise made validation rankings more faithful on every panel. Yet on the prospective external panel, cover agreement with the exact validation winner rose from 90.5% to 95.8% while selected test loss became worse by 2.93 × 10−3 on average. The exact validation and test winners agreed on only 2 of 8 datasets.

This is not a side note. A noisy selector can accidentally choose the test-preferred model; a better selector faithfully chooses the validation-preferred one. Across alternate splits, candidate-gap movement was at least 2.51 times—and typically 55.3 times—the remaining cross-score RMSE scale. Once nuisance integration is nearly solved, data-partition instability is the larger problem.

What survived Day 5

01Choose the estimand first

The target is the full-pipeline quotient over declared exact equivalences and seed—not whichever spelling happened to run.

02Balance known structure

Spend fits covering nuisance interactions. Match cover strength to the measured fANOVA spectrum.

03Respect the boundary

Lower nuisance variance improves evaluation. It cannot guarantee that one finite held-out split ranks models correctly.

Day 5 conclusion. Exact schema choices and random seeds should be treated as one finite nuisance product. OrbitCover turns that declaration into an equal-fit estimator, and independent packed covers turn the estimator into an unbiased selection score. The contribution is not the classical ingredients alone; it is their complete-pipeline composition.

The paper direction is now OrbitCover. It is an evaluation and model-selection paper, not a predictive-SOTA claim. The next decisive experiment is an untouched multi-source panel with repeated data splits, because the remaining uncertainty no longer comes mainly from how the schema orbit is sampled.
