Better neighbors don't always make a better crowd.

Retrieval models predict by gathering nearby examples. But judging each neighbor alone can punish combinations that become excellent when averaged.

August 31, 2026Han Joon Byun

On Day 7, an average had to declare what it averages. Day 8 found the same issue inside retrieval models: a score for one neighbor is not automatically a score for a group of neighbors.

The lesson. Evaluate the prediction the model actually makes. If it averages 16 neighbors, measuring 16 isolated one-neighbor predictions can diagnose the wrong mechanism.

A crowd can cancel its mistakes

Imagine estimating a value of 10 from two neighbors. One suggests 13; the other suggests 7. Each misses by 3, so both look poor alone. Average them and the signed errors cancel exactly. A one-at-a-time score throws that useful pairing away.

Noise behaves differently too. If several independent neighbors are averaged, their noise is diluted by their squared weights. This gives the exact risk of the aggregate:

Under ordinary nonnegative averaging, the one-neighbor score is an upper bound; its gap is the cancellation and dilution it ignores.

The sensible idea that failed

I tested a natural intervention: estimate how unreliable each training example is, then steer TabR and ModernNCA away from risky candidates. Across 12 real datasets, the reranker lowered its one-neighbor proxy risk on 11/12 datasets for TabR and 9/12 for ModernNCA. Yet predictive score improved by only 0.00033 for both—less than a control that randomly permuted the reliability labels.

The intervention moved the ruler. The outcome barely moved.

This is more informative than “the method did not work.” The theory was correct for one chosen neighbor; the measurement was wrong for a multi-neighbor predictor. Opposite conditional-mean errors were cancelling, and averaging was already suppressing much of the candidate noise.

Fixing the ruler changed the story

A post-hoc correction optimized the aggregate formula directly. It produced a real dataset-balanced gain of about 0.018 for both models. But the ablations reversed the proposed explanation: using only mean mismatch matched the full method, while reliability alone hurt TabR and was nearly neutral for ModernNCA.

The corrected method also missed its preset transfer gate for ModernNCA on the synthetic noise test: 5 of 8 seed wins, short of the required 6. Because the positive result was designed after seeing the first failure, it remains a lead—not confirmation. The Day 8 method search stops here.

Audit an aggregate as an aggregate. A mean of individual risks answers a different question.

02Ablate the story

The full method improved, but mismatch—not candidate reliability—carried the gain.

03Let gates stop you

A useful post-hoc signal earns a new prospective test, not a retroactive victory.

Day 8 conclusion. Better retrieval is not simply a bag of better neighbors. The prediction depends on how their signed mistakes and noise combine. The durable result is the corrected measurement—and the discipline to stop when the proposed mechanism does not survive it.
