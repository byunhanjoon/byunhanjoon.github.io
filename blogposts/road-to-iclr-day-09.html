Turning the paper shouldn't change the answer.

Turn the graph paper without moving the dots. A person sees the same pattern. An AI model may give a different answer.

September 1, 2026Han Joon Byun

Imagine a scatterplot of patients, homes, or companies. Now turn the paper slightly. No dot moves relative to another. A person immediately knows it is the same picture. But when I gave this kind of harmless rewrite to AI models for tables, their answers often changed.

The simple lesson. A dependable model should learn the pattern of the dots, not which way the graph paper happens to face.

Turn the paper, change the answer

A table may describe the same facts in many ways. We can measure temperature in different units, rename categories, or combine two columns into new directions. If the change is reversible, no information has disappeared. It is like rotating a map: north moves on the page, but the roads and distances stay put.

This was not a quirk of one model or one table. I tried five very different kinds of models on 11 real datasets. Nearly three quarters of the model–dataset pairs changed enough to matter. Even ordinary alternative encodings—not only artificial rotations—produced the same problem.

The direction of the axes was influencing the answer.

Why would a model care?

Training a model is like walking downhill through a landscape. Some training rules take different-sized steps in different compass directions. Turn the compass, and the route can change—even when the hill itself does not.

To check this explanation, I made two models begin with exactly the same predictions. A simple training rule kept them together after the grid turned. A widely used adaptive rule gradually pulled them apart again. The difference was being created during learning, not by missing information.

Navigate by landmarks, not compass directions

A natural fix is to stop telling the model where a row sits on arbitrary axes. Instead, describe how it relates to a few familiar examples from the training data—like giving directions from landmarks rather than from “east” and “north.” Turn the map and those relationships remain the same.

THE LANDMARK VIEWnew row → relationships to familiar rows → stable descriptionturn the map; the landmarks stay in the same places relative to one another

The landmark view made the model perfectly stable—but sometimes less accurate. That matters. The original directions, although arbitrary, can act as useful shortcuts for learning. Throwing them away everywhere traded one kind of unreliability for another.

So the final method kept both views. One model reads the original table; another reads the landmark version. A small practice set decides how much to trust the stable view and returns to the original whenever the change looks risky.

Keep the stable view when it helps. Step back toward the original when it does not.

This is not a universal cure. It needs two trained models and still lost some comparisons. But it passed the most important common-sense test: the typical answer became much less sensitive to the direction of the graph paper, without hiding a disastrous failure elsewhere.

Day 9 conclusion. Reliable AI should care about the people and patterns in a table, not the direction of its invisible graph paper. The best answer was not to ignore coordinates completely. It was to read the table through stable landmarks while keeping the original view close enough to catch what the landmarks miss.
