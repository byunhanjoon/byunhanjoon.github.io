Road to ICLR · Day 9 / 30

The same table shouldn't need lucky coordinates.

Rotate a feature space and every row, distance, and label can remain unchanged. Modern tabular models often do not.

September 1, 2026Han Joon Byun

Day 3 showed that equivalent coordinates can create different learning problems. Day 9 asked a stricter question: does this still happen under perfectly conditioned rotations, across current model families, and can we remove it without removing useful inductive bias?

The lesson. Invariance is not free. A coordinate-free interface can erase arbitrary sensitivity, but it can also erase a useful learning bias. The safer target is controlled invariance: use as much as validation can justify, then fall back to the raw view.

Nothing changed—except the prediction

Take a numerical feature block X and multiply it by an orthogonal matrix Q. The new coordinates XQ are exactly invertible; distances and angles are preserved; the condition number is one. This is the same data in a rotated basis.

The preference was broad. Across 11 development datasets and five model families—CatBoost, MLP, TabM, TabICL, and TabPFN—40 of 55 dataset–model units crossed the preregistered disagreement threshold. Median disagreement was 7.67%. Recognizable alternatives such as one-hot versus Helmert and local versus spectral spline bases also disagreed, with a 5.66% median.

The optimizer rebuilt the difference

Perhaps the two runs simply began as different functions. I removed that explanation by matching their initial predictions. With function-matched SGD, the two bases stayed equivalent. With AdamW, disagreement grew back to 8.42%. Adaptive coordinate-wise updates were reconstructing a coordinate preference that the function itself did not require.

This does not make AdamW “wrong.” Coordinates choose an optimization geometry, and that geometry can sometimes help. The experiment says only that the resulting basis dependence is real, mechanistic, and not guaranteed to be benign.

Exact invariance was the wrong finish line

A Gram interface describes an embedded row through inner products with fixed training anchors. Rotate both the row and anchors and the coordinates become identical:

ROTATION-INVARIANT INTERFACE(ZQ)(AQ)T = ZQQTAT = ZATarbitrary axes disappear; similarities to anchors remain

Pure Gram features removed 100% of rotational disagreement, but the average hid dangerous tails. On the completed embedding panel, their 95th-percentile normalized excess risk was 10.87%, and the worst cell reached 333%. A fixed 75% Gram mixture was better, but its worst cell still reached 169%. Exact invariance had become a metric victory and a predictive-risk failure.

The final completed test added a simple guard. Fit raw and invariant branches, then choose the largest Gram weight among 75%, 50%, 25%, and 0% whose validation cost stays below a fixed 1% budget. On 12 untouched datasets and three neural backbones after RBF embedding, the guarded method cut median basis disagreement by 75%, improved task risk in 29 of 36 units, and kept the worst observed normalized excess risk to 0.64%.

The guard does not prove a universal solution. It uses two fits, covers orthogonal changes inside numerical embeddings, and still loses 6 of 36 units. But it resolves the sharpest failure of the earlier method: the median improvement no longer conceals a catastrophic observed tail.

Day 9 conclusion. The same table can become a different predictor merely by rotating its coordinates. A Gram interface can remove that accident, but the raw basis sometimes carries useful inductive bias. The best result was therefore not perfect invariance. It was a guarded compromise that controlled the orbit without trusting it blindly.
